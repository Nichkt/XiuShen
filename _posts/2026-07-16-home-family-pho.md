## Pre-recipe considerations

### Picking the beef

You should pick pieces that are cheap. This is a working class meal. Pho made with wagyu and filet will lack heart and context — it won't taste right, or good.

The beef pieces should have bone and connective tissue along with some meat.

- Tendon meat is the best mix of price, taste, and texture, but hard to find in usual grocery stores. Chinese or Vietnamese markets might have it.
- Beef shank is good. Costco sells this now in packs of three. Not a bad choice.
- Ribs or rib pieces are good to use, but need to be taken care of carefully. The ends tend to splinter into bone shards.
- Neck bones are OK, but need to be treated more carefully because they may splinter into bone shards very commonly.
- Beef knuckle bones are great.
- Brisket flats are great and cheap if you can buy for under $6/lb.
- Short ribs used to be ideal, but now they're too expensive. If you can find the racks of short ribs sold for $7/lb and cut them yourself, that may be worth doing.
- Oxtail is good but annoying to eat, and too expensive — it won't be cheaper than short rib. This is just an influencer thing now, for people cooking for people's eyes, not their mouths and stomachs.

If you want to do thin slices of raw beef, you can. I skip it — it's a lot of hassle. Just buy a cheap cut like eye of round or brisket. Don't fall for white people garbage and start using filet or ribeye here. It's to be cut thin and cooked fast. You don't need it to be super tender. Using a poached shank or shin muscle is fine.

### Picking the spices

Use whole spices. Don't use the powders. You don't know what powders are adulterated with. Ginger is cheap; powdered ginger has a high chance of being cut with lead or other heavy metals by merchants. Same with other powdered spices. It's not worth it.

The spices are:

- Coriander seed (can be skipped, but you should have it — some restaurants will skip)
- Cumin seed (not traditional, but I like it)
- Fennel seed (not traditional, but I like it)
- Star anise (Chinese), use 2 or 3
- Cardamom, black — seeds are good, or if using the pod, just toss the pod in the pot whole
- Cinnamon stick — Saigon is fine, Mexican cinnamon is good too. Break it into small pieces for your spice holder
- Cloves — don't use too much, can be overpowering. One or two is enough
- Rock sugar

Where to buy: Chinese or Vietnamese grocery stores. Korean or Japanese groceries will have these too, but pricier with no increase in quality. Indian groceries will also have all the necessary spices, sometimes at higher quality — depends on the neighborhood. Go to an actual grocery store, not a corner store or bodega. Packets and cans of these spices last a long time, so it's not worth buying old.

### Fish sauce

Fish sauce is the base of the soup, and this is what gives the warm feeling. It must be a good fish sauce. Three Crabs is a good brand. Red Boat is a great brand. Use a Vietnamese brand — other countries like Thailand (Squid brand) add a lot of other ingredients.

What to avoid:

- Do not use a fish sauce with added MSG — disodium inosinate or disodium guanylate, "I+G," is super MSG. This isn't a knock against MSG generally, it just doesn't belong here. It's a mark of low-quality garbage in the context of fish sauce.
- Do not use a fish sauce with added coloring agents.
- Try to buy one without sugar. You can add sugar yourself — you don't need it premixed in.

Traditional high-quality fish sauce usually has only two ingredients: anchovies and salt.

Brand notes:

- The lowest quality sauce to tolerate is Three Crabs, even with added sugar — this is for a soup, you aren't eating it straight or using it as a dipping sauce. It's OK.
- Viet Huong brand is fine — this is Three Crabs. They also have another brand with a lion on it.
- The absolute worst thing you can do is buy "Thai Kitchen." This isn't because it's a Thai brand — it's because it's McCormick. It is irredeemable trash. Avoid all other occurrences of this brand too, from curry pastes to anything else they make. It will all be garbage.

### Picking the herbs and vegetables

Cilantro or culantro (ngò or ngò gai). Culantro is better here but harder to find. Cilantro can be found anywhere — best to buy it from a Chinese or Vietnamese store. H Mart is OK.

Thai basil — don't use regular basil. The stems will be purple, the leaves skinnier.

### Picking the noodle type

Any rice noodles will do. Some people like vermicelli. Traditional noodles are bánh phở. Don't use ones that are too thin, and noodles that are too thick will take annoyingly long to prepare.

### Considerations to make it better

- Purge the beef before souping it.
- Toast the spices.
- Char the onion and ginger, either in a pan or the oven.
- Do not boil this. If it boils, it will taste worse than if it hadn't. Hard rolling boils are for Korean soups — Vietnamese soup looks for a clear broth instead of something cloudy. Boiling also makes the meat stringier and less tasty, because the intermuscular fat and collagen escape.
- Do not use an instant pot or pressure cooker — it will create trash. This soup should be simmered, not boiled, or the meat will be dry. Put it on the stove, use an induction cooker, or make a fire outside and tend to it.

### A note on MSG

Most restaurant pho uses MSG — it's cheaper. If restaurant pho is what you're used to eating, you'll want to add a little yellow rock sugar to mimic the sweetness MSG lends. It has no place in home made pho made in the western world that you are serving to yourself and your family. If you make this correctly, you don't need MSG. I don't care how it's used in other foods — for home made pho, it's a crutch for uncaring or bad cooks.

### A note on ginger

Pick older ginger if possible. It has more flavor. You aren't candying this — you want the flavor of the ginger to really come through.

### Pot size

Don't bother if you have only a small pot. Have at least 6 quarts.

## Recipe

### Ingredients

- Pot
- Beef
- Water
- Ginger
- Onion
- Spice bag or reusable tea bag, filled with:
  - Coriander seed
  - Cumin seed
  - Fennel seed
  - Star anise
  - Cardamom
  - Cinnamon
  - Clove
- Sugar
- Rice noodles
- Thai basil
- Cilantro or culantro
- Another onion — green onions are good, sweet onion is nice
- Bean sprouts
- Hoisin sauce
- Lime wedge
- Sriracha

### Instructions

1. Soak the beef for a while — the longer you do, the more blood and protein liquid leaks out. That's a good thing.
2. Put the beef into a pot of water and boil for a couple minutes, long enough for the scum to leave the bones. Don't boil for too long — not 15 minutes, that's too long. You don't want the meat to cook here, just to remove the scum.
3. Pour all of it out. Wash each piece of beef by hand to make sure there's no scum left. Wash the pot to remove any scum stuck on the walls and bottom.
4. Place the beef back in the pot and pour in drinking water --- this will be the water you drink with the soup, so if you used tap water before, switch to filtered water now. If you don't have a water filter, get one, like a ZeroWater. Brita is trash, don't use a Brita.
5. Put the pot on low. This should not boil again, ever --- at most it should simmer. The more it boils, the more quality you lose in beef texture. You aren't making korean seolleongtang. you are making Pho. don't boil it. This is also why pho made in an instant pot is trash, because pressure cooking is high-temperature cooking. It wrings the beef dry. 
6. Peel and char both ginger and onion — about a finger length of ginger. Slice the ginger so it has more surface area and leaches flavor into the water better. The onion can be quartered. Char it in a pan or toaster oven.
7. Put the ginger and onion in the pot with the beef.
8. Prepare the spices: a couple pinches of coriander, cumin, and fennel seed. Two or three star anise pods — you can break the spikes off to make them fit. If your cardamom is in pod form, just toss it in the pot; if not, toss it in a nonstick pan with the rest. Break the cinnamon into small pieces. The cloves can be left whole.
9. Put all the spices into a pan to toast. Keep the heat moderate, but hot enough. Don't burn the spices — just warm them enough to activate their oils. You'll smell it when it's done. Pay attention, don't walk away, or you'll make garbage.
10. Bundle the toasted spices into a tea infuser or spice infuser and put it into the pot. You'll fish it out later — you don't want these spices floating around freely, they aren't nice to find in a bowl.
11. The soup is essentially done now. Keep it on low and leave it alone for a couple hours. Add a little rock sugar if you want — no more than one or two small rocks. It will smell nice but won't taste very good until you add fish sauce. You don't need to add fish sauce yet — add it toward the end of the simmer, to taste. A couple glugs of the bottle should get it where you want. The amount is personal taste, but err lighter — you can always add more. Salt will also come from the hoisin later, so add enough fish sauce that the soup tastes warm but not salty.
12. Simmer for hours. Don't worry about it.
13. While waiting, prep the herbs so you don't have to right before eating. Wash and chop the Thai basil and cilantro/culantro leaves. If using cilantro, save the stems for braising meats later in the freezer. Keep the herbs separate, don't mix them. Slice some onion thinly as well.
14. Wash the bean sprouts.
15. When ready to eat, boil rice noodles in plain water until soft. Portion the cooked noodles into bowls.
16. In a smaller pot, scoop out some beef and soup from the larger pot and warm it with a handful of sliced raw onion and the herbs — Thai basil and cilantro. Once the beef is hot, it's done, and ready to portion into the noodle bowls.
17. Add the bean sprouts to the bowls, not to the pot.
18. Serve with hoisin and sriracha. Taste the soup before adding sauces — it's really annoying watching people add sauce before trying the soup. How do you know how much to add? How do you know what it tastes like, or how salty or sweet it is? Taste the bowl before you add anything.


### Story
That's the recipe. It's how I'll be making this for you. It's how I make it for myself and have made it for years. Things will change over time like cuts of beef, whatever is cheaper at the time or the types of noodles, whichever is on sale. It'll taste great. 

I don't have measurements. You'll have to figure it out like I did. That's what'll make it personal to you. Good luck buddy. You can't follow recipes word for word with exact measurements, the people who do never really make great food. 

I hope you like cooking. I've never really understood people who don't. You cannot really love eating food without liking making it. You're going to eat every day of your life. Enjoy it. Make it a good experience. Have some opinions on how it should be done and standards to hold yourself to so that you and the people you like can eat well.
